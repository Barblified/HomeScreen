Infrastructure and utilities

MODERATE · STABLE Confidence: medium-high · Horizon: days–months.

What changed: Ofgem opened a consultation on activating its market stabilisation charge, reintroduced in May after wholesale-price increases related to the Iran conflict. The mechanism compensates suppliers that hedged in advance if wholesale prices fall sharply and customers switch to cheaper tariffs. [1]

Why it matters: The proposal is a supplier-resilience measure for a volatile winter; it is not evidence of imminent shortage and Ofgem says activation would not automatically raise bills.

Evidence / uncertainty: Trigger levels, notice periods and deactivation arrangements are still under consultation.

Practical implication: Keep routine provision for short outages and monitor tariff information, but do not change supplier or buy equipment solely because of the consultation.

Cyber and communications

ELEVATED · STRATEGICALLY RISING Confidence: high · Horizon: immediate–months.

What changed: No attribution-backed national disruption was identified. The Bank of England is nevertheless working with banks on deep, bare-metal recovery because defences cannot be assumed impenetrable against AI-enabled attacks. [9]

Why it matters: For households, fraud, credential theft, payment interruption and supplier outages remain more probable than direct sophisticated targeting.

Evidence / uncertainty: Resilience planning signals credible concern but is not evidence that a systemic incident is under way.

Practical implication: Use unique passwords and MFA, preserve offline recovery codes and keep an alternative payment and communications plan.

MODERATE · IMPROVING SLOWLY Confidence: medium-high · Horizon: months.

What changed: Ofgem's winter-market proposal adds a current operational resilience measure to ongoing financial-sector cyber-recovery work.

Why it matters: Supplier stability and recoverability of core banking infrastructure reduce the chance that shocks become prolonged service failures.

Evidence / uncertainty: Both initiatives are preparatory: the Ofgem design is not final, and cyber-recovery capability remains partly untested at system scale.

Practical implication: Treat institutional resilience as one layer, not a replacement for proportionate household continuity.

Practical household and EDC preparedness

MODERATE PRIORITY · STABLE Confidence: high · Horizon: now.

What changed: No development justifies expanding beyond an ordinary multi-day continuity baseline.

Why it matters: The credible scenarios are short power, water, payment, communications or transport interruptions—not societal collapse.

Evidence / uncertainty: UK Prepare guidance advises considering supplies for an emergency lasting a few days and recommends at least 2.5–3 litres of drinking water per person per day for survival. [10]

Practical implication: Prefer reliable, low-dependency equipment already owned; fill genuine gaps gradually.

Correlated risks

Energy-market volatility → supplier and tariff pressure → household-budget strain → weaker emergency savings.
Hybrid or AI-enabled cyberattack → financial or communications interruption → temporary payment and information-access problems.
Local flood or severe weather → transport and power interruption → short-lived household isolation.

No evidence indicates that a nationwide cascade is under way.

EDC / preparedness check

  • Already covered: charged phone, identification, weather layer and ordinary payment method.
  • Check: power bank and cable, compact torch, radio batteries, medicines, first aid and vehicle visibility equipment.
  • Check: smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms; preserve supplier numbers and account-recovery codes offline.
  • Consider: hygienically rotated drinking water and a receive-only battery radio where absent.
  • Avoid: anonymous batteries, unnecessary duplicates and equipment dependent entirely on an app or cloud service.
